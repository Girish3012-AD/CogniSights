# PS26143 Oil Spill / Vessel Attribution — technical landscape

## The core problem, decoded, and why it's really two problems
1. **Detection**: find oil slicks in SAR imagery. Solved-adjacent — mature, published, high-accuracy open pipelines exist.
2. **Attribution**: given a detected slick, name the vessel most likely responsible, using AIS (Automatic Identification System) tracking data. This is the hard, differentiating half, and it is NOT solved-adjacent — it's an active research and operational problem run today by professional maritime-safety agencies with resources your team won't have in a week.

Given NTRO (an intelligence/attribution-focused agency, not an environmental-monitoring one) is the sponsor, the attribution half is very likely the actual point of the PS, not a nice-to-have on top of detection.

## Detection half — datasets and models (all public, mature)
- **OSD / M4D dataset** (Krestenitis et al., 2019) — built from Sentinel-1 SAR, EMSA CleanSeaNet-derived; 1,002 training + 110 testing images, five classes (oil-spill, look-alike, land, ship, sea surface), 650×1250 resolution. Original release: Zenodo, https://zenodo.org/records/3497086 ("Oil Spill Identification from Satellite Images Using Deep Neural Networks"). This is the standard, most-cited SAR oil-spill segmentation dataset — start here.
- **Deep-SAR Oil Spill (SOS) dataset** (Zhu et al.) — 8,070 labeled 256×256 SAR patches from ALOS PALSAR (Gulf of Mexico) and Sentinel-1A (Persian Gulf), cropped from 21 raw scenes. Referenced across multiple 2024-2026 papers (arXiv:2412.08116, arXiv:2503.12404) as the second standard benchmark alongside OSD/M4D.
- **LADOS** (2025, Zenodo 15888341) — aerial/UAV imagery dataset for oil spill detection with finer class granularity (general oil, emulsion, sheen); useful if you want a non-SAR optical angle too, but SAR is what actually matters for a satellite-based PS.
- **Model choices**: DeepLabv3+ is the most consistently cited top performer across recent papers (one 2025 study on the Suez Canal reports 98.14% accuracy, 0.79 MIoU using DeepLabv3+ on a combination of EMSA-CSN and localized data — DOI: 10.1038/s41598-025-03028-1). U-Net and SegNet variants are also well-documented and lighter to train if compute/time is tight. SAM2-based approaches (OilSAM2, SAM2-ELNet) are 2025-2026 state of the art if you want a newer story than "we trained a U-Net."
- **Practical read**: detection is very achievable at high accuracy in days, not the full week, using off-the-shelf DeepLabv3+ or U-Net on OSD/M4D + SOS. Don't over-invest engineering time here — it's table stakes, not your differentiator.

## Attribution half — the part that actually matters, and the part that's hard
This is where the real prior art lives, and it's institutional, not a GitHub repo you can clone:
- **Longépé et al. (2015), "Polluter identification with spaceborne radar imagery, AIS and forward drift modeling"** — defines the actual operational method used by European agencies: rather than backtracking a detected slick to a past position (fragile, since drift models degrade going backward), the method **forward-simulates** drift for every AIS-tracked vessel in the area and compares each simulated trail against the observed slick to rank candidate polluters. This is the correct conceptual approach if you want to be scientifically honest, and it's more tractable to approximate than naive backtracking. Source record: agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65dea4454c5aef494fdcfe8a.
- **EU JRC, "Satellite Monitoring of Illicit Maritime Pollution: Backtracking Towards Source Identification"** (JRC52276, published 2010) — documents a real case study combining satellite-derived spill area, HF-radar-measured currents, and AIS shipping data for backtracking/attribution. Confirms this exact capability has been operational at agency level (EMSA/JRC) for over a decade. Source: https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/handle/JRC52276.
- **What this means for novelty**: you cannot claim "we invented vessel attribution from SAR+AIS" — it's an 15-year-old operational concept at the EU level. Your legitimate novelty claim is narrower: an accessible, India-focused, explainable/uncertainty-quantified version of this method, not the underlying concept.
- **What this means for feasibility**: a full hydrodynamic hindcast/forward-drift model (real ocean currents + wind fields + oil weathering physics) is out of reach for a 1-week student build without an oceanography specialist on the team. A credible, honest SIH-scale approximation is: simple wind-drift vector estimation (oil drifts roughly downwind/with-current at a known fraction of wind speed — this is a textbook approximation, not the full physics) + proximity/time-window scoring of AIS tracks near the slick's estimated origin, presented explicitly as a simplified ranking model with confidence bands, not a definitive accusation.

## AIS data — the actual bottleneck
- **AISHub** — community AIS data-sharing network; free tier available but requires you to also contribute a receiving station or partner with a member to get meaningful historical coverage; check current terms before assuming free bulk historical access.
- **MarineTraffic API** — has a free/trial tier but historical density and geographic coverage for Indian waters specifically needs verification; paid tiers are the realistic route to real historical data at usable density.
- **Practical read**: for a demo, you likely cannot get dense, free, real-time or deep-historical AIS for Indian coastal waters in a week without either paying for API access or a contact with institutional access (DG Shipping, Indian Coast Guard, or a paid MarineTraffic/Spire/exactEarth tier). Plan for a synthetic-but-realistic AIS overlay for the demo (generate plausible vessel tracks consistent with real Indian shipping lanes) and be explicit about this in the pitch — judges will respect honesty about data provenance far more than an unlabeled synthetic dataset presented as real.

## What a credible 1-week build actually looks like
1. Train/fine-tune DeepLabv3+ or U-Net on OSD/M4D (+ SOS if time allows) for slick segmentation — 1-2 days, well-trodden path.
2. Build a simple wind-drift/proximity attribution scorer: given a detected slick's location/time, pull (real or synthetic) AIS tracks in a surrounding time-distance window, rank vessels by a transparent scoring function (proximity to estimated origin point, consistent heading/speed, time alignment), output a ranked candidate list with confidence — NOT a single named "responsible vessel."
3. Build a map-based UI (slick overlay + candidate vessel tracks + confidence scores) — this is your visual demo moment.
4. Write an explicit, one-slide "how attribution works and its limitations" explanation — given the legal/reputational weight of naming a vessel, this slide is not optional, it's what separates a credible team from a naive one in front of NTRO judges.

## Where this can go wrong
- Presenting a single "responsible vessel" claim with unstated confidence is the single biggest risk in front of judges — always present ranked candidates with explicit uncertainty.
- If your AIS data is synthetic and this isn't disclosed, a judge asking "where's this AIS feed from" mid-demo is a credibility-ending moment. Disclose upfront instead.
- Full physical drift modeling (real ocean currents, oil weathering/evaporation rates) is genuinely out of reach in a week without a domain specialist — don't overclaim scientific fidelity you don't have; a simplified, clearly-labeled approximation is the honest and still-impressive choice.
